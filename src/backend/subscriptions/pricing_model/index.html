Credit & licensing economics

Turn one dial — the price of a credit — and watch it fall through the whole model: what each tool costs to run and charges, how many credits a plan must include, what that plan has to charge to hold its margin, what a self-serve team is worth, and what an enterprise pool earns per credit. Baselines are the live values in the code; every field is editable.

▲
Prices are BASE RATES now (2026-08-17). The charge is max(base, metered): credit_prices holds the published price of the deliverable and acts as a floor, and the token-derived amount only wins when a run costs more than the base already covers (~$0.20 of COGS at a 30-credit base — roughly 20× a median run). So the base rate sets the effective markup, and CREDIT_MARKUP below now only decides where the outlier tail begins. Read tab 1's per-action COGS as the tail boundary, not as the price. A cache hit measures $0 and still pays the base, deduped to one charge per deliverable per billing period.

Two numbers exist for every run: the base (a flat price you set) and the metered figure (what the tokens the run actually burned would cost at the margin %). The toggle above decides how they combine. Base is a floor — what the backend ships — charges whichever is higher: an ordinary run pays exactly the base, and only a run whose tokens cost more than the base already covers is charged more. Base + tokens charges both every time, so the advertised price is never what a run actually debits. Under the floor rule a tool's effective margin (the number in each card header) is set by its base rate, not the margin dial; blank margin inherits the default. Cache hits charge the base too — the deliverable is identical; only Zoe's greeting/thanks fast-path is free. Live base rates are credit_prices; the margin dial is one global CREDIT_MARKUP today — per-tool values here are a spec for a backend change.

Price needed is what the plan must charge to hit the target margin at full grant consumption — the worst case, where a subscriber burns everything they are entitled to. How a team pool is funded and spent

Every inflow lands in the pool's RESERVE — purchased credits never expire

built Ways credits enter

  • Transfer from your own reserve. Personal reserve → pool via transfer_credits (409 on insufficient, never a silent clamp). Only reserve moves — packs and admin grants — never the monthly plan grant, which stays personal and expires.
  • Buy a pack into the pool. Same ladder as personal packs, checkout on the admin's card, lands in reserve.
  • Recurring top-up. The same pack on a monthly Stripe subscription riding the buying admin's personal customer (kind='org_topup'). Offboarding that admin cancels it.
  • Nothing else. No dispersal, no overage, no pay-as-you-go on a pool. A dry pool means only an admin buying credits helps — a member sees no CTA.

built How it's spent

  • Members hold no wallet. They spend straight from the pool, bounded by a monthly cap (org_members.monthly_cap → org default 2,000 → -1 = no limit).
  • Caps are ceilings, not reservations. They may sum past the pool; the pool bounds spend, the caps only ration it.
  • Over-cap debits are flagged, never refused (charge-on-success). capReached → the member asks an admin to raise the cap; approving moves nothing.
  • Standing is a daily predicate. An owner short on slots starts a 14-day grace on the extra teams, then they lapse and the whole subtree is denied. Archive frees a slot and keeps the pool; dissolve returns the artists and forfeits the reserve.

The structure, as drawn vs as built

built What the code does now

  • One pool, per-member caps. Members hold no wallet; they spend from the org pool bounded by monthly_cap (falling back to the org default, then uncapped).
  • The cap moves inside the debit. debit_credits takes the pool lock and the member row together, so two concurrent actions can't both slip under the ceiling. An over-cap debit is recorded and flagged, never rejected — charge-on-success means the work already happened.
  • Monthly dispersal expires. The sweep rolls the pool each period: this month's volume lands in the expiring bucket, purchased packs sit in reserve and survive.
  • Request → approve raises a cap. No credits move, so approving is idempotent by nature and costs the pool nothing until someone spends.
  • Offboarding reclaims nothing — there was never a balance to take back, just a ceiling.

still open What the contract wrapper needs

The credit mechanics are built; the commercial terms are not. There is no org subscription object, so the 12-month term, the monthly payment, the cancellation grace period and the "remaining months still owed" rule live in the contract you sign, not in the app — an admin buys credits or an operator sets the dispersal.

Also unbuilt: time-gated permissions (only invites expire, after 48 hours), and role-gated payment drafting (payouts carry draft/paid but nothing restricts who confirms).

Same as Google's calculator with min instances = 1: a warm instance is billed every second of the month — at the idle CPU rate ($0.0000025 / vCPU-s) while it waits and the active rate ($0.000024) while it serves, memory at $0.0000025 / GiB-s either way, plus $0.40 per million requests. Always-on (instance-based billing) is a flat $0.000018 / vCPU-s + $0.000002 / GiB-s with no request charge — cheaper only when the service is busy most of the time. This lands as a cost line in the Contract P&L below and in every margin figure above; the fee is what you add to the monthly payment to cover it.

Where the modeled counts come from

  • OneClick cold: extraction + basis + split-verify on gpt-5.2, plus a mini payable-column detect.
  • OneClick warm: extraction served from the shared parse cache — the two advisory passes are the whole bill.
  • Zoe: routing + answer + citations on mini, escalating to gpt-5 for multi-contract questions.
  • Registry parse: one extraction call, capped at CONTRACT_PARSE_MAX_CHARS (250k ≈ 96 pages).
